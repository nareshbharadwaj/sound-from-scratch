How Sound Becomes Numbers

Every speech and music model works on lists of numbers. This lesson shows where those numbers come from, and the one way the process can trick your ears.

About 30 minutes. No math background needed: each idea comes with a picture or a sound. Headphones help.

1

What sound is

When a guitar string vibrates, it pushes the air next to it back and forth. That push travels outwards as tiny ripples in air pressure. When the ripples reach your ear, your eardrum moves with them, and you hear sound.

A microphone does the same job as your eardrum: it measures the air pressure, moment by moment. If you draw that measurement over time, you get a waveform. Up means the air is slightly squeezed, down means it's slightly stretched, and the middle line is still air.

Key idea: sound is a quantity (air pressure) that changes over time. A waveform is just a graph of it.
2

Frequency: how fast it wiggles

The simplest sounds repeat the same shape over and over. The number of repeats per second is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). 440 Hz means 440 repeats every second.

Your ear hears frequency as pitch: more repeats per second sounds higher. The height of the wave, its amplitude, is heard as loudness. People hear roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz; the top end drops with age.

Try it: count the bumps at 100 Hz, then at 400 Hz. The picture always shows 10 milliseconds (a hundredth of a second), so a 100 Hz wave fits exactly 1 repeat, and 400 Hz fits 4.

3

The sine wave

The smooth wave in step 2 is a sine wave, and it's the building block of every other sound. You can make one with a circle: put a dot on a spinning wheel and track only how high the dot is. Plotted over time, that height draws a sine wave.

One trip round the circle is one cycle of the wave. Mathematicians measure a full turn as 2π (about 6.28) instead of 360°. So a sine wave written as a formula is:

height = A × sin(2π × f × t)
  • tthe time, in seconds
  • f × thow many cycles have happened by time t. At 440 Hz, after half a second, 220 cycles.
  • 2π ×turns cycles into an angle on the circle, since one cycle is one full turn
  • sinthe height of the dot at that angle, between −1 and 1
  • Athe amplitude, which scales the height
Key idea: a sine wave is a point going round a circle. Frequency is how many turns per second.
4

Sampling: turning a wave into numbers

A computer can't store a smooth curve. Instead it measures the wave's height at evenly spaced moments, like a camera taking photos at a steady rate. Each measurement is a sample, and the number of samples per second is the sample rate.

Below is a 500 Hz tone. The dots are the samples. The list underneath is everything the computer keeps; the smooth curve is thrown away.

The numbers the computer stores

Real audio uses fast sample rates: 16,000 per second for many speech models, 44,100 for CDs. One second of CD audio is 44,100 numbers.

Key idea: digital audio is a list of heights, measured at a fixed rate. Nothing between the dots is kept.
5

How many samples are enough?

If the computer only has the dots, can it tell what wave they came from? Here the sample rate is fixed at 8,000 per second. Raise the frequency and watch how many dots land on each cycle.

The real waveSamplesDots joined up

To show a wave going up and down, you need at least one sample near each peak and one near each dip: at least 2 samples per cycle. So a sample rate can only capture frequencies below half of itself. That limit is called the Nyquist frequency.

  • 8,000samples per second captures up to 4,000 Hz
  • 16,000captures up to 8,000 Hz, which is enough for clear speech
  • 44,100(CD) captures up to 22,050 Hz, just past the limit of human hearing. That's why CDs chose it.

At exactly 4,000 Hz the slider shows something odd: every sample can land on the middle line, and the wave vanishes. That's why the limit is strictly below half the sample rate.

Key idea: a sample rate can record frequencies up to half its value, the Nyquist frequency. Anything higher can't be told apart.
6

The wagon-wheel trick

So what happens to a frequency that's too high? You've probably seen the answer in films. Cameras take 24 photos per second. A wheel spinning fast can look like it's turning slowly, or even backwards. Each square below is one photo of a wheel with one spoke.

At 23 turns per second, the wheel turns almost a full circle between photos, so each photo shows the spoke slightly behind where it was. Your brain joins the photos up into the simplest motion: slowly backwards. The camera never sees the fast motion that actually happened.

7

Aliasing: the same trick with sound

Sampling is a camera for sound, so the same thing happens. Take a 7,000 Hz tone and sample it 8,000 times a second, which is above the 4,000 Hz limit. Between samples the wave goes through 7/8 of a cycle: almost a full turn, just like the wheel. The dots line up into a slow wave instead.

The real toneSamplesThe wave the dots trace

Before tapping play: what pitch will 7,000 Hz sound like? Then compare it with 1,000 Hz. They sound identical, because the computer stored the same numbers. This disguise is called aliasing: a too-high frequency shows up as a false, lower one, its alias.

Key idea: frequencies above Nyquist don't disappear. They come back disguised as lower frequencies, and nothing in the samples can tell you which one was real.
8

The rule

The wheel gives the rule away. A wheel turning 24 times per second looks frozen, because each photo catches it in the same place. So adding or removing 24 turns per second changes nothing in the photos. For sound, adding or removing the sample rate changes nothing in the samples.

So to find what you'll hear, slide the frequency up or down by whole sample rates until it's as close to zero as possible. Then ignore the sign: a wheel turning backwards at 1 turn per second looks just as slow as one turning forwards.

heard = distance from f to the nearest multiple of the sample rate

At 8,000 samples per second, the multiples are 0, 8,000, 16,000 and so on. A 7,000 Hz tone is 1,000 away from 8,000, so you hear 1,000 Hz. A 9,000 Hz tone is also 1,000 away from 8,000: also 1,000 Hz. Predict these, then reveal:

A 5,000 Hz tone, sampled 8,000 times a second.

Show answer

The nearest multiple is 8,000, which is 3,000 away, so you hear 3,000 Hz. Just above the 4,000 Hz limit, the frequency folds back to just below it.

A 12,000 Hz tone, sampled 8,000 times a second.

Show answer

12,000 is 4,000 away from both 8,000 and 16,000, so you hear 4,000 Hz, right at the limit.

A 20,000 Hz tone on a CD, sampled 44,100 times a second.

Show answer

A trick question: 20,000 Hz is below the CD's 22,050 Hz limit, so it's stored correctly and you hear 20,000 Hz, if your ears can still reach that high.

Written as code, which is what alias_frequency does in the repo:

heard = abs(f − sample_rate × round(f / sample_rate))

round(f / sample_rate) picks the nearest multiple, the subtraction measures the distance, and abs drops the sign.

9

Why it matters for speech and music models

  • LimitsEvery model works at a fixed sample rate, and can never produce anything above half of it. A 16,000 Hz speech model can't make sounds above 8,000 Hz, which is part of why some synthetic voices sound slightly muffled.
  • ArtifactsModels that build audio step by step from a coarse sketch can accidentally create too-high frequencies, which fold back as faint metallic whistles. Vocoder papers in phase 4 work hard to avoid this.
  • ResamplingConverting audio to a lower sample rate means first removing everything above the new limit. Skip that, and the high frequencies alias.

Back to the lab

You now have every idea the Aliasing Lab uses. Here's what its parts show:

  • Tone → Heard as: the frequency you chose, and its alias from step 8.
  • What the samples see: the picture from step 7, showing 24 samples. Orange is the wave the samples trace.
  • The fold: across the bottom, the tone you choose; up the side, the pitch you hear. The line rises until Nyquist, then folds back down to zero at the sample rate, then rises again, like a strip of paper folded into a zigzag. The blue dot is your current tone.
  • Sample rate buttons: a higher rate moves the first fold further right, so more frequencies are stored correctly.
