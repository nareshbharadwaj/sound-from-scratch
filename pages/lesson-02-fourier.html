Taking Sound Apart

Lesson 1 turned sound into a list of numbers. This lesson turns that list into a recipe: which pure tones it's made of, and how loud each one is. The tool that does it is the Fourier transform.

About 40 minutes. Builds on lesson 1: sine waves, sample rate and Nyquist. Each new idea comes with a picture or a sound.

1

Sounds are sine waves added up

A single sine wave sounds pure and plain, like a tuning fork. Real sounds are richer because several sine waves are happening at once. When two waves meet in the air, their pushes simply add: if one pushes up by 0.3 and the other pulls down by 0.1, the air moves up by 0.2.

For samples this is just as simple. To mix two sounds, add their lists number by number: sample 1 plus sample 1, sample 2 plus sample 2, and so on. Below are the three notes of an A major chord. Turn them on and off and watch the thick line, their sum.

Each note on its ownThe mix you hear

With all three on, the mix looks like a mess. Your ear still hears three clean notes, because your inner ear separates sound by frequency. The rest of this lesson builds a machine that does the same with numbers.

Key idea: mixing sounds means adding their samples. A complicated waveform can be a few simple sine waves added together.
2

Going backwards: the spectrum

Adding sines is easy. The hard direction is the reverse: someone hands you the messy list of samples, and you have to say which sines are inside and how loud each one is. It's like tasting a smoothie and naming the fruit and how much of each went in.

The answer is drawn as a spectrum: frequency along the bottom, and up the side how strong each frequency is. Here is the spectrum of the chord from step 1. It follows the notes you switched on above.

The waveform and the spectrum describe the same sound in two ways. The waveform says how the air moves over time. The spectrum says how much of each frequency there is. The Fourier transform is the method for getting from the first to the second. The next steps build it from one simple trick.

Key idea: a spectrum lists the frequencies in a sound and their strengths. The Fourier transform computes it from the samples.
3

The matching trick

How do you find out if a signal contains, say, 300 Hz? Make a test wave at 300 Hz and compare. Multiply the signal by the test wave, sample by sample, then average the products. That average is the match score.

Why does this work? Where both waves are up, the product is positive (up times up). Where both are down, it's positive too (minus times minus). So if the signal moves in step with the test wave, the products are all positive and the average is big. If it moves at a different speed, the waves drift in and out of step, positive and negative products balance, and the average comes out to zero.

The signal below is a single 300 Hz tone. Before you move the slider: which test frequency do you expect to give a big score?

Positive productsNegative products

At exactly 300 Hz the score is 0.5, not 1. That's because sin × sin swings between 0 and 1, so it averages to one half. Doubling the score gives back the tone's amplitude, so from now on scores are doubled: a score of 1 means "this frequency is present at full strength".

Key idea: multiply by a test wave and average. Matching frequencies add up; every other frequency cancels out.
4

The timing problem: phase

The trick has a flaw. A wave doesn't have to start at zero going up. It can start at a peak, or anywhere else in its cycle. Where a wave is in its cycle at time zero is its phase, measured as an angle round the circle from lesson 1: 0° to 360°.

Shift the signal by 90° and it starts at its peak. Now it's up exactly when the test sine is crossing the middle, and the score drops to zero, even though the frequency matches perfectly. Try it.

Signal (300 Hz)Test sineTest cosine

The fix is to test with two waves at the same frequency: the sine, and a cosine, which is the same wave started a quarter turn (90°) earlier, at its peak. When one score drops, the other rises.

Plot the two scores as a point: sine score across, cosine score up. As you change the phase, the point travels round a circle. Its distance from the centre stays the same, and that distance is the strength of the frequency. Its angle is the phase. It's the circle from lesson 1 again: a sine wave really is a point going round a circle, and two match scores find exactly where that point is.

Key idea: match against a sine and a cosine. The two scores together give the strength (distance from the centre) and the phase (the angle), whatever the timing.
5

Test every frequency

Now we can take apart a sound we don't know. Here is a mystery sound, 20 milliseconds of it. Listen, then guess how many tones it contains.

To find out, run the matching trick (sine and cosine) at every test frequency from 0 to 1,000 Hz, and plot the strength each one finds. Tap Scan to watch the curve build up, or drag the slider to test one frequency at a time.

Three peaks: tones at 150, 400 and 650 Hz, with amplitudes 0.6, 0.35 and 0.2. That's the recipe, read straight off the curve. This curve is the spectrum from step 2, computed rather than drawn by hand.

Look between the peaks, though. The curve doesn't sit flat at zero. It ripples, and only touches zero at certain frequencies: 50, 100, 200, 250 Hz, every multiple of 50. The next step explains why.

Key idea: scan the matching trick across frequencies and you get the spectrum. Peaks show which tones are in the sound and how loud they are.
6

Which frequencies to test: bins

Our 20 ms clip at 8,000 samples per second is 160 samples long. A 50 Hz wave fits exactly one full cycle into 20 ms. A 100 Hz wave fits exactly two, 150 Hz exactly three, and so on. Those are the frequencies where the curve hit zero.

Test waves that fit a whole number of cycles have a special property: they cancel each other perfectly. Any two different ones multiply and average to exactly zero, which is what you saw in step 3. So a tone sitting on one of them shows up there and nowhere else. These whole-cycle frequencies are called bins, and they're the only frequencies the Fourier transform tests.

Change the length and notice that the test waves don't change shape: bin 1 is always one cycle across the clip. What changes is how many hertz that is. The gap between bins is one over the clip's length: 20 ms gives 50 Hz steps, a full second gives 1 Hz steps. Listen longer and you can tell closer frequencies apart. This is called the frequency resolution.

How many bins? From 0 up to Nyquist, which is half the number of samples, plus one. Each bin has two scores (sine and cosine), and the two end bins only need one. That makes exactly N numbers out for N samples in. Nothing is gained or lost: it's the same information, rearranged.

Key idea: with N samples, the bins are the frequencies that fit 0, 1, 2 … N/2 whole cycles. They sit sample_rate ÷ N apart, which is one over the clip's duration.
7

Tones between bins: leakage

Real sounds don't politely sit on bins. What happens to a tone that doesn't fit a whole number of cycles? Below, the clip is 20 ms again, so bins are 50 Hz apart. The tone starts at 325 Hz, halfway between the 300 and 350 Hz bins. Predict first: what will its spectrum look like?

The tone's strength spills out over many bins. The peak is shorter than the true amplitude, and bins far away pick up a bit too. This is spectral leakage. It happens because the tone ends partway through a cycle, so its cancellation against every test wave is no longer perfect. Slide to 300 or 350 Hz and the leakage vanishes.

Leakage is why lesson 1's dominant_frequency multiplies the samples by a smooth hump called a window before matching. Windows are the start of lesson 3.

Key idea: a tone between bins leaks into its neighbours. The spectrum shows it blurred, centred on the right frequency.
8

Putting it back together

Each bin gives a frequency, a strength and a phase. That's everything you need to draw a sine wave. So the trip can be reversed: draw every bin's sine wave and add them up, as in step 1. That gives back the original samples exactly. Going back is the inverse Fourier transform.

Here's a buzzy sound, a sawtooth wave at 110 Hz, shown dashed. Its spectrum has a bar at 110 Hz, 220, 330 and every multiple, each weaker than the last. Rebuild it one sine at a time. Before you slide: will it sound brighter or duller as you add waves?

Target sawtoothSum of the sines so far

With one wave you hear a pure 110 Hz hum. Each extra wave adds a higher frequency, and the sound gets brighter and buzzier. Pitch stays the same, because the first wave sets the repeat rate. The higher waves set the timbre, the colour of the sound. That's why a violin and a flute playing the same note sound different: same first wave, different recipe above it.

Key idea: the spectrum loses nothing. Adding all the bins' sine waves back up rebuilds the samples exactly.
9

The formula

Everything so far, written down. Number the samples n = 0, 1, … N−1 and the bins k = 0, 1, … The test waves for bin k make k cycles across the N samples, so at sample n they are at angle 2π·k·n/N round the circle. The two match scores for bin k are sums of signal × test wave:

cos score[k] = Σ x[n] × cos(2π·k·n / N)
sin score[k] = Σ x[n] × sin(2π·k·n / N)
  • x[n]sample number n
  • k·n / Nhow many cycles the test wave has made by sample n
  • 2π ×turns cycles into an angle, as in lesson 1
  • Σ"add up over every n". A sum rather than an average: we don't divide by N

Papers pack the two scores into one complex number. Don't let the name worry you: a complex number is just a pair of numbers, an across part and an up part, so it's a point on a flat plane, like the point on the circle in step 4. The letter i marks the up part. In that notation, the point on the circle at angle θ is written eiθ, which is shorthand for cos θ + i sin θ. So the whole transform becomes:

X[k] = Σ x[n] × e−i·2π·k·n / N
  • X[k]bin k's result: a point whose across part is the cos score and whose up part is minus the sin score
  • |X[k]|its distance from the centre: the strength. For a sine of amplitude A sitting on bin k, it's N × A ÷ 2
  • angleits angle: the phase
  • −the minus sign is a convention, so test waves turn the opposite way round the circle. Flipping it flips every phase, nothing else

This is the discrete Fourier transform, or DFT. "Discrete" because it works on samples rather than a smooth curve. The inverse adds the waves back, with the sign flipped and a 1/N to undo the sum:

x[n] = (1/N) × Σ X[k] × e+i·2π·k·n / N

Check yourself before revealing:

You record 1 second of speech at 16,000 samples per second. How far apart are the bins, and how many are there from 0 to Nyquist?

Show answer

Gap = 16,000 ÷ 16,000 = 1 Hz. Bins run from 0 to 8,000 Hz, so there are 8,001 of them.

A sine of amplitude 0.5 fits whole cycles into 1,000 samples. What is |X[k]| at its bin?

Show answer

N × A ÷ 2 = 1,000 × 0.5 ÷ 2 = 250. Libraries return this raw sum, so code that wants amplitudes multiplies by 2/N, as amplitude_spectrum does.

With a 20 ms clip, can the DFT tell a 410 Hz tone from a 420 Hz tone?

Show answer

Barely. The bins are 50 Hz apart, so both tones fall between the 400 and 450 Hz bins and leak in similar ways. To give them separate bins you'd need 10 Hz spacing, which means a clip of at least 100 ms.

10

Making it fast: the FFT

Done directly, the DFT multiplies every sample by every bin's test wave: N × N steps. For one second of speech, that's 16,000 × 16,000, about 256 million. Speech models compute thousands of these per training example, so the direct way is far too slow.

The fast Fourier transform (FFT) gets the same numbers with a trick of splitting in half. Take the even-numbered samples on their own. A test wave making k cycles across all N samples, looked at only at the even samples, is a wave making k cycles across N/2 samples. So the evens need only a DFT half the size. The odd-numbered samples are the same, except each one comes one sample later, which just turns their result by a fixed angle round the circle. Two half-size DFTs, plus a quick step to combine them, give the full answer.

Then split each half again, and again, until the pieces are single samples:

N samples can be halved log2N times (8 → 4 → 2 → 1 is 3 times, and 23 = 8). Each level of splitting costs about N steps to combine. So the FFT takes about N × log2N steps instead of N × N. The halving works best when N is a power of 2, which is why you see sizes like 512, 1,024 and 2,048 everywhere in audio code.

Key idea: the FFT is not a different transform. It computes exactly the DFT, by splitting the samples into evens and odds over and over. N × N steps become N × log2N.
11

Why it matters for speech and music models

  • SpectrogramsSpeech changes from moment to moment, so models run the FFT on short clips one after another, giving a picture of frequency over time. That's lesson 3, and it's the input or output of most TTS models.
  • ResolutionStep 6's tradeoff never goes away: short clips follow fast changes but blur frequencies; long clips separate frequencies but blur timing. Every model picks a clip length, usually 20–50 ms.
  • PhaseModels often keep only the strengths and throw the phase away. Step 8 needs both to rebuild sound, so something has to guess the phase back. That's Griffin-Lim later in this phase, and the vocoders of phase 4.
  • LossesVocoders and codecs are trained to make the spectrum of their output match the real one, using FFTs inside the training loss.
