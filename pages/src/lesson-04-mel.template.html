Hearing Like an Ear

A spectrogram spaces its rows evenly in hertz. Your ears don't hear that way: they're fussy about low notes and relaxed about high ones. This lesson bends the spectrogram's frequency axis to match, which gives the mel spectrogram, the picture most speech models read and write.

About 40 minutes. Builds on lesson 3: the spectrogram, decibels, harmonics and formants, and rebuilding sound with random phase. Headphones help, especially for the high notes and the voice.

Same gap, different jump

Here are two pairs of notes. In each pair the second note is exactly 100 Hz higher than the first. The gap in hertz is the same.

Predict first

Will the two pairs sound like the same size of jump? If not, which one sounds bigger?

After you've listened

The low pair is an obvious jump, about the gap between the first two notes of "Here Comes the Bride". The high pair barely moves: you can hear that the second note is a touch higher, but only just.

What your ear notices is the ratio between the notes. 400 ÷ 300 is 1.33: the second note is 33% higher. 4,100 ÷ 4,000 is 1.025: just 2.5% higher. The same 100 Hz is a big step low down and a tiny one up high.

Key idea: ears judge the distance between pitches by their ratio, not their difference in hertz.
2

A ladder that sounds even

Musicians have known this for centuries. Going up an octave means doubling the frequency: 110, 220, 440, 880 Hz are all the note A, each one octave higher. On a piano the keys are evenly spaced, but each key is about 6% higher than the one before, so the frequencies are spaced by ratios.

Here are two ladders of eight notes, both from 300 Hz to 6,600 Hz. The first climbs in equal steps of hertz: 900 Hz each time. The second climbs in equal ratios: each note is 1.56 times the one before. The dots show where each note sits.

The first ladder starts with a huge leap, two whole octaves, then its steps shrink until the last few sound almost the same. The second ladder's notes crowd together at the bottom and spread out at the top, yet each step sounds about as big as the last.

Key idea: to sound evenly spaced, notes must be packed close together low down and spread far apart up high.
3

The mel scale

So a ruler for pitch should count ratios. Hearing research refined that idea into the mel scale (from "melody"). In the 1930s researchers played people a tone and asked them to set a second tone to sound half as high. From the answers they built a scale where equal distances sound equal. Its unit is the mel, and it's pinned so that 1,000 Hz is 1,000 mels.

The curve below converts hertz to mels. Move the slider and watch how much 100 Hz is worth at different heights. The dashed line is what the curve would be if mels were just hertz.

Above about 1,000 Hz the curve flattens: 100 Hz is worth less and less, and each octave adds a similar number of mels, as on the piano. Below 1,000 Hz the curve stays near the dashed line: there, mels are close to plain hertz, and 100 Hz is worth roughly 100 mels wherever you are. So the mel scale counts ratios up high and hertz down low.

The reason is in the ear itself. Inside the cochlea, a coiled tube in the inner ear, a membrane runs along its length, and each spot on it shakes most for one frequency. Up high, each octave gets about the same length of membrane, so ratios are what count. Low down, the spacing is closer to even in hertz. The mel scale is a rough map of that membrane.

Key idea: the mel scale spaces pitch the way the ear does: close to plain hertz below 1,000 Hz and close to counting ratios above.
4

Where a spectrogram spends its rows

Now look at lesson 3's spectrogram of the voice through this lens. It has 512-sample frames at 16,000 samples per second, so 257 rows from 0 to 8,000 Hz, each row 31.25 Hz above the last. The rows are evenly spaced in hertz.

Predict first

To your ear, 4,000–8,000 Hz is a single octave, the same size of step as 300–600 Hz. How many of the 257 rows does the spectrogram spend on it?

Show the answer

129 rows, half the picture, go to the top octave. Everything below 1,000 Hz, where the voice's pitch and its first formant live, gets 32. The picture spends most of its detail where your ear has the least.

Key idea: evenly spaced rows give high frequencies far more detail than hearing uses, and low frequencies comparatively little.
5

Bands instead of rows

The fix is to regroup the rows into bands that are evenly spaced in mels: narrow ones low down, wide ones up high. Each band is a triangle. It counts the rows near its centre fully and the rows near its edges a little, and its edges reach the centres of its neighbours, so no frequency falls between bands. A band's value is the weighted average of the rows under its triangle. The set of triangles is a mel filterbank.

Pick how many bands, and slide to pick one. Then switch the axis to mels.

On the hertz axis the triangles widen as they climb. On the mel axis they're all the same: equal slices of pitch as you hear it. The bars at the bottom compare how the plain rows and the bands spread over the octaves. Speech models nearly always use 80 bands.

Key idea: a mel filterbank is a row of overlapping triangles, evenly spaced in mels. Each one averages the spectrogram's rows beneath it into one number.

One frame: 257 rows

The same frame: 80 bands

Zoomed in, the lowest bands are narrow enough that each harmonic, each peak 200 Hz apart, gets bands of its own, so the bands still show the peaks. High up, one band swallows several harmonics at once and only the overall shape, the formants, survives.

Key idea: 257 numbers per frame become 80. Low bands keep the harmonics apart; high bands keep only the outline.
7

The mel spectrogram

Do that for every frame and stand the results side by side, as in lesson 3, and you get a mel spectrogram: 80 rows instead of 257, with the rows evenly spaced in mels.

Predict first

In lesson 3's spectrogram, her harmonic stripes fill roughly the bottom fifth of the picture. In the mel spectrogram, what happens to them: same size, bigger, or blurred away?

Show the mel spectrogram
−70 dB0 dB

The stripes grow to fill about half the picture, and the lowest ones stay sharp: the mel picture gives the region your ear cares most about far more room. Above about 2,500 Hz the stripes melt into smooth bands, because each mel band there is wider than the 200 Hz between harmonics. The formants, the bright regions, are still there. The hiss at the top shrinks to a few rows.

Like lesson 3's pictures, this one is in decibels. Models use the same idea in a slightly different unit: the plain natural logarithm of each band's value, with tiny values raised to a floor first so silence doesn't become minus infinity. That's called a log-mel spectrogram.

Key idea: a mel spectrogram is a spectrogram with its rows regrouped into mel bands. It gives low frequencies room and summarises high ones.
8

What the bands throw away

Squeezing 257 rows into 80 loses detail. How much can you hear? To listen to a mel spectrogram, first spread each band back over the rows it covered: every row takes the value of the bands over it, blended by the triangles' heights. Detail inside a band can't come back.

Then the strengths need phases. As in lesson 3's step 9, the picture has none, so every version below gets the same random phases. That's why even the 257-row version sounds rough. Compare each one to it, not to the original.

Predict first

Going down from 257 rows to 80, 40, 20 and 10 bands: when does her pitch fade, leaving a whisper? And will 80 bands sound much worse than 257 rows?

Show the experiment

What the rebuild starts from

80 bands sound almost the same as all 257 rows: the detail they drop is detail your ear barely uses. At 40 the voice is a little duller. At 20 the low bands get too wide to keep the harmonics apart, the stripes vanish, and the pitch starts to fade. At 10 it's a whisper, yet most of the words are still there, carried by the formants and the timing.

The pitch strength above measures how closely a short stretch of sound matches itself shifted by one buzz of the vocal cords: near 1 for a clear, steady pitch, near 0 for noise. It's measured on the vowel from lesson 3's step 7.

Key idea: 80 mel bands keep nearly everything the ear needs. Too few bands lose the harmonics, and with them the pitch.
9

The formula

The mel scale most code uses comes from the HTK speech toolkit:

mel(f) = 2595 × log10(1 + f ÷ 700)
  • f ÷ 700Below 700 Hz this is small, and log(1 + small) is almost the small number itself, so mels grow in step with hertz.
  • log₁₀Well above 700 Hz the "1 +" hardly matters, so mels grow with log(f): every doubling adds the same amount.
  • 2595Scales the result so 1,000 Hz comes out at 1,000 mels.

To build B bands between fmin and fmax, space B + 2 points evenly in mels and turn them back into hertz: e0, e1, … eB+1. Band b rises from 0 at eb to 1 at eb+1 and falls back to 0 at eb+2. Writing F[b, k] for band b's weight on row k, scaled so each band's weights add up to 1, the mel spectrogram is

M[b, m] = Σk F[b, k] × |X[k, m]|

where |X[k, m]| is lesson 3's spectrogram: row k, frame m. Models then take ln(max(M, 0.00001)).

Check yourself before revealing:

How many mels is 700 Hz?

Show answer

2595 × log10(2) = 781 mels. 700 Hz is where f ÷ 700 = 1, the middle of the bend.

80 bands from 0 to 8,000 Hz (2,840 mels). How far apart are the band centres, in mels? And roughly in hertz near the bottom?

Show answer

2,840 ÷ 81 = 35 mels. Near 0 Hz a mel is about 0.62 Hz, so the centres are about 22 Hz apart there. Up near 7,000 Hz they're about 240 Hz apart.

A common text-to-speech setting is 22,050 samples per second, a hop of 256 and 80 bands. How many numbers per second does a model have to produce, compared with the samples themselves?

Show answer

22,050 ÷ 256 = 86 frames a second, × 80 bands = about 6,900 numbers a second, against 22,050 samples. Three times fewer, and each one changes smoothly, which makes them much easier to predict.

10

Why speech models use it

  • TargetTacotron 2 turns text into an 80-band log-mel spectrogram, one frame every 12.5 ms. A vocoder then turns the mel spectrogram into sound. Phase 5 builds one.
  • VocoderHiFi-GAN takes those 80 bands and invents everything step 8 couldn't: the phases and the fine detail inside each band. Phase 4 builds one.
  • LossVocoders and codecs compare mel spectrograms of their output and the real audio, so mistakes count roughly as much as a listener would notice them.
  • ListeningSpeech recognisers like Whisper read 80 log-mel bands too: 25 ms frames every 10 ms at 16,000 samples per second.

Words from this lesson

Ratio
One frequency divided by another. Equal ratios sound like equal steps.
Octave
A doubling of frequency. Notes an octave apart share a name.
Mel scale
A ruler for pitch where equal distances sound equal. Close to hertz below 1,000 Hz, close to counting ratios above.
Cochlea
The coiled tube in the inner ear where each spot responds to one frequency.
Band
A range of frequencies grouped into one number.
Mel filterbank
A row of overlapping triangles, evenly spaced in mels, that turns a frame's rows into bands.
Weighted average
An average where some values count more than others. Here, rows near a band's centre count most.
Mel spectrogram
A spectrogram with its rows regrouped into mel bands. Usually 80.
Log-mel spectrogram
The natural log of a mel spectrogram, with a floor for silence. What most speech models read and write.
