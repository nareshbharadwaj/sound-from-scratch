Seeing Sound Over Time

Lesson 2's spectrum says which frequencies are in a sound, but not when they happen. This lesson slices a recording into short pieces and stacks their spectra into a picture of sound over time: the spectrogram. At the end you'll read a real voice in it.

About 40 minutes. Builds on lesson 2: bins, leakage, phase, and the fade from exercise 5. Headphones help for the voice.

1

A spectrum can't tell time

Here are two sounds, each about a second long. Sound A plays a low note and then a high one. Sound B plays the same two notes the other way round. To your ear they're obviously different. Listen to both.

Before you look at the figure: lesson 2's Fourier transform turns each sound into one spectrum. Will the two spectra be different?

Spectrum of A

Spectrum of B

Identical. The spectrum adds up the whole clip at once: it says there's some 400 Hz and some 800 Hz, and nothing about which came first. For speech that's useless. A word is a sequence of sounds, and the order is the word.

Key idea: one spectrum of a whole recording tells you what frequencies are in it, but not when.
2

Listen through a short slice

The fix is to stop looking at the whole clip. Cut out a short piece, 32 milliseconds here, and take the spectrum of just that piece. Each short piece is called a frame. Drag the slider to move the frame along sound A.

Early in the clip the frame only sees 400 Hz; late in the clip, only 800 Hz. Park it right on the switch at 0.51 s and it sees a bit of both. A frame is like a glance: it only knows about the moment it covers.

Key idea: take the spectrum of a short frame and you learn what frequencies are present at that moment.
3

Soften the cut: windows

Cutting out a frame has a cost you met in exercise 5. The frame starts and stops abruptly, usually partway through a cycle, so its spectrum leaks: the strength smears out over bins far from the real note.

The cure is the same as in exercise 5: fade the frame in and out before taking its spectrum. The fade shape is called a window. The usual one, the Hann window, is a smooth hump: zero at both edges, one in the middle. Each sample of the frame is multiplied by the hump's height at that point.

Frame of the 400 Hz noteWindow shape

With the fade, the peak gets a little wider but the smear far away almost disappears. That's a good trade: in a spectrogram of speech, a quiet sound next to a loud one stays visible, as the hidden 1,500 Hz tone did in exercise 5.

Key idea: fade every frame in and out with a window. It stops the cut edges from smearing the spectrum.
4

Overlap the slices

Fading has its own cost. Near the edges of each frame the window is almost zero, so whatever happens there barely counts. If frames sit end to end, sounds that fall between them are nearly ignored.

So frames overlap. The step from the start of one frame to the start of the next is called the hop. The figure shows a row of Hann windows and, as a thick line, their total: how much each moment of the sound counts overall.

Each frame's windowTotal weight

With a hop of half a frame, the humps fill each other's gaps exactly and the total is flat: every moment counts the same. A quarter-frame hop is flat too, with more frames per second. Speech models usually hop a quarter of a frame.

Key idea: overlap the frames so that, added up, the windows give every moment of the sound the same weight.
5

Stack them up: the spectrogram

Now put it together. Slide a windowed frame along the sound one hop at a time, take each frame's spectrum, and stand the spectra side by side like slices of bread. Time runs along the bottom, frequency up the side, and the colour of each spot shows how strong that frequency is at that moment.

The whole procedure is the short-time Fourier transform, or STFT. The picture it makes is a spectrogram. Here are sounds A and B again. Tap play and watch the line move across.

Each note is a horizontal bar: one frequency, held for a while. Now A and B look as different as they sound. The bars are a little thick because each frame's bins are 31 Hz apart and the Hann fade widens the peak, as in step 3.

Key idea: the STFT slides a windowed frame along the sound and takes the spectrum of each. The spectrogram shows frequency over time.
6

A real voice, and decibels

Here's 2.6 seconds of a woman reading a novel aloud, from a public collection of audiobook recordings. It's recorded at 16,000 samples per second, so the picture goes up to 8,000 Hz, the Nyquist limit from lesson 1.

weakstrong

With plain strength the picture is nearly empty: a few bright blobs low down and little else. Speech mixes very loud parts with very quiet ones, and on a plain scale the quiet ones vanish next to the loud ones. Tap Decibels.

The decibel (dB) scale counts by factors of ten instead of by steps. Every −20 dB is 10 times weaker: 0 dB is the loudest spot, −20 dB is a tenth of it, −40 dB a hundredth, −80 dB a ten-thousandth. Your ears hear loudness in this way, so decibels show the voice the way you hear it. In symbols:

dB = 20 × log10(strength ÷ loudest)
Key idea: real sounds span huge ranges of strength. Spectrograms are drawn in decibels, where each step of 20 dB is a factor of 10.
7

Reading a voice

Everything in this picture comes from ideas you already have. Tap each feature to outline it, then play just that part.

  • StripesVowels and voiced sounds. The vocal cords buzz like lesson 2's sawtooth, so the sound is a stack of harmonics: the lowest frequency and its multiples, here about 200 Hz apart. When her pitch rises or falls, the whole stack bends together.
  • BandsInside the stripes, some regions are brighter than others. These are formants: frequencies the shape of the mouth and throat make louder. Moving the tongue and lips moves the formants, and that's what makes "ee" sound different from "ah" at the same pitch.
  • HissSounds like s, sh and f have no buzz at all, just air rushing through a narrow gap. That's noise: a smudge across many high frequencies, with no stripes.
  • GapsShort silences inside words, often where the lips or tongue close off the air for a moment before a sound like p, t or k. The release can show as a thin vertical line: a click, which contains every frequency at once, as in lesson 2's click experiment.
Key idea: a voice spectrogram is readable. Stripes are the buzz of the vocal cords, bright bands are the mouth's shape, smudges are hiss, and gaps are closures.
8

Short slices or long ones?

How long should a frame be? Lesson 2 gave the rule: bins are one over the frame's duration apart. A long frame gives fine frequency detail but blurs anything that changes quickly. A short frame catches quick changes but can't separate nearby frequencies. Predict what happens to the stripes at 4 ms and at 128 ms, then try it.

At 4 ms the stripes vanish into vertical smears: the bins are 250 Hz apart, wider than the gap between harmonics. At 128 ms the stripes are razor sharp, but the starts and ends of sounds smear sideways, and the bending stripes blur. Speech systems settle on about 20–50 ms, where both are good enough. You can't have perfect detail in time and frequency at once. This is the time–frequency tradeoff.

Hear the tradeoff for yourself in Two Close Tones: two notes 20 Hz apart that short frames see as one pulsing note and long frames see as two steady ones.

Key idea: frame length trades time detail for frequency detail. Every spectrogram is a choice somewhere in between.
9

From picture back to sound

Can you turn a spectrogram back into sound? If you kept everything, yes. Each frame's spectrum goes back to a frame of samples with lesson 2's inverse transform. Fade each one with the window again, lay them back at their positions and add up the overlaps. This is the inverse STFT, and it rebuilds the recording exactly.

But a spectrogram picture keeps only strengths. Each bin also had a phase (lesson 2, step 4), and the picture throws it away. What would the voice sound like if we rebuilt it from strengths alone, with every phase replaced by a random angle? Predict, then listen.

With phase kept, the rebuild matches the original to within rounding. With random phase, the words are still there but the voice turns rough and hollow, like a robot whispering through a fan. In each frame the waves no longer line up the way they did, and neighbouring frames no longer agree with each other.

This matters later. Many text-to-speech models predict only a spectrogram of strengths. Something then has to invent phases that sound right. Griffin-Lim, later in this phase, does it by trial and improvement; the neural vocoders of phase 4 learn to do it.

Key idea: the full STFT can be undone exactly. A spectrogram of strengths alone can't: the phase is lost, and has to be rebuilt.
10

The formula

Everything above in one line. Frame m starts at sample m × H, where H is the hop. Multiply its N samples by the window w, then take the DFT from lesson 2:

X[m, k] = Σn x[m·H + n] × w[n] × e−i·2π·k·n / N
  • mwhich frame: a column of the spectrogram, at time m × H ÷ sample_rate
  • kwhich bin: a row, at frequency k × sample_rate ÷ N
  • x[m·H+n]sample n of frame m
  • w[n]the window's height at sample n
  • e…bin k's test wave, as in lesson 2

The Hann window is one cycle of a cosine, flipped and lifted so it runs from 0 up to 1 and back:

w[n] = 0.5 − 0.5 × cos(2π·n / N)

The spectrogram is |X[m, k]| in decibels. Three numbers describe any spectrogram: the frame length N, the hop H and the window. A common setting in text-to-speech is N = 1,024 and H = 256 at 22,050 samples per second: 46 ms frames, a new frame every 11.6 ms (86 per second) and 513 bins.

Check yourself before revealing:

At 16,000 samples per second, a hop of 200 samples gives how many frames per second?

Show answer

16,000 ÷ 200 = 80 frames per second, one every 12.5 ms.

A 25 ms frame at 16,000 samples per second has how many samples, and how far apart are its bins?

Show answer

0.025 × 16,000 = 400 samples. Bins are 16,000 ÷ 400 = 40 Hz apart, one over 25 ms.

A man's voice has harmonics about 100 Hz apart. What's the shortest frame that can separate them?

Show answer

Bins must be closer than 100 Hz, so the frame must be longer than 1 ÷ 100 Hz = 10 ms. In practice about twice that, because the Hann fade widens each peak to about two bins.

Why it matters for speech and music models

  • The targetClassic text-to-speech models write a spectrogram one frame at a time, then a vocoder turns it into sound. Lesson 4 bends the frequency axis to match human hearing: the mel spectrogram these models actually use.
  • PhaseStep 9's robotic whisper is the problem vocoders exist to solve.
  • LossesVocoders and codecs compare spectrograms of their output and the real audio at several frame lengths at once, because step 8 showed no single length sees everything.
  • MusicA piano note is a stack of stripes with a sharp vertical start; drums are mostly vertical lines. The same picture reads music too.

Words from this lesson

Frame
A short piece of a recording, analysed on its own.
Window
A fade shape multiplied into each frame so its cut edges don't smear the spectrum.
Hann window
The usual window: a smooth hump, 0 at the edges and 1 in the middle.
Hop
The step from one frame's start to the next. Usually a quarter or half of the frame.
STFT
Short-time Fourier transform: the spectrum of every windowed frame, one hop apart.
Spectrogram
The STFT's strengths drawn as a picture: time across, frequency up, colour for strength.
Decibel (dB)
A scale that counts strength by factors of ten: every 20 dB is 10 times stronger.
Harmonics
A buzz's lowest frequency and its whole-number multiples. In a spectrogram, stacked stripes.
Formant
A band of frequencies the mouth and throat make louder. Formants set which vowel you hear.
Time–frequency tradeoff
Longer frames sharpen frequency and blur time; shorter frames do the reverse.
Inverse STFT
Turning every frame back into samples and adding the overlaps. Exact only if the phase is kept.
