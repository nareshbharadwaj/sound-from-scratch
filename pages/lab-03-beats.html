Two Close Tones

Lesson 3's time–frequency tradeoff, made audible. Two tones 20 Hz apart, played together, then drawn with short, medium and long frames.

1

Listen

First each tone on its own, then both at once.

Predict before you play the last one: will "both together" sound like two notes, one note, or something else?

Show what's happening
Both tones addedHow loud the mix is

You hear one note, about 1,010 Hz, that swells and fades 20 times a second: a fast "wah-wah-wah". These pulses are called beats.

They come from the two tones drifting in and out of step, like the two runners. When they line up, their pushes add and the sound is loud. Half a lap later they're opposite and cancel, and the sound almost vanishes. The faster tone gains a full cycle every 1 ÷ 20 Hz = 50 ms, so this repeats 20 times a second. Musicians tune by listening for beats: they slow down as two strings get closer, and stop when they match.

4 ms: one thick band, flashing 20 times a second. The frames are much shorter than the 50 ms beat, so they catch every swell and fade. But the bins are 250 Hz apart, so the two tones sit in the same bin and look like one.

32 ms: still one band, still pulsing, but blurrier in both directions. Bins are 31 Hz apart, more than the 20 Hz gap, and the frames are getting close to the beat's length.

256 ms: two steady lines, at 1,000 and 1,020 Hz. Bins are 4 Hz apart, so the tones separate cleanly. But each frame spans five whole beats, so the pulsing averages away.

3

Both pictures are right

Here's the surprise. "One 1,010 Hz note pulsing 20 times a second" and "two steady notes at 1,000 and 1,020 Hz" aren't two guesses where one is wrong. They're the same sound, described two ways. A trigonometry identity says so exactly:

sin(2π·1000·t) + sin(2π·1020·t)
= 2 × cos(2π·10·t) × sin(2π·1010·t)

The left side is two steady tones. The right side is one 1,010 Hz tone whose loudness is scaled by a slow cosine at 10 Hz. Its peaks and troughs both count as loud, so you hear 20 swells a second.

Short frames show the right-hand version; long frames show the left. The frame length doesn't make one description wrong. It chooses which one you see. That's the time–frequency tradeoff in its purest form: you can know when the loudness changes, or which exact frequencies are there, but no single frame length shows both.

Key idea: two close frequencies and one pulsing frequency are the same sound. Short frames see the pulsing; long frames see the two frequencies.
